## Level 1: Mechanical Design

### 1. Introduction to CAD

Computer-Aided Design (CAD) is used to create, modify and analyse engineering models digitally before manufacturing. I learned how sketches, dimensions, geometric constraints and 3D modelling operations are used to develop accurate components. I also understood the role of parametric modelling, where changes to dimensions update the associated geometry. CAD helps reduce manual drafting effort and improves design accuracy during product development.

### 2. Autodesk Fusion 360

I explored the Fusion 360 interface and its design environments, including Solid, Surface, Sheet Metal, Form and Assembly tools. I learned how 2D sketches are converted into 3D geometry using operations such as Extrude, Revolve, Sweep and Loft. The software also supports joints, motion studies, technical drawings and parameter-driven modifications, making it useful for modelling individual components as well as complete mechanical systems.

### 3. Engineering Drawing — Orthographic Projections

I studied orthographic  projections to represent three-dimensional components through engineering drawings. Orthographic projection uses front, top and side views to communicate dimensions, holes, profiles and other geometric features.I learned how these views complement each other in communicating component geometry and manufacturing requirements.

### 4. Generating 2D Drawings in CAD

I explored the creation of 2D engineering drawings using sketches, geometric constraints and dimensioning tools. I learned how horizontal, vertical, coincident, parallel and perpendicular constraints control sketch geometry. I also understood the importance of consistent dimensions, centre lines, hidden lines and suitable projection views. These features help produce drawings that can be used for component inspection and manufacturing.

### 5. 2D Drafting — Sierpinski Triangle

I explored the construction of a Sierpinski Triangle using repeated triangular divisions. The pattern is generated by dividing a triangle into smaller triangles and recursively removing the central triangular region. This exercise introduced me to geometric repetition, symmetry and self-similarity. It also helped develop accuracy in sketch construction and the use of repeated geometric operations.The Sierpinski Triangle is used in computer graphics, architecture, decorative designs and 3D modelling to create complex patterns through repeated geometric shapes. It is also useful in mathematics for understanding recursion, self-similarity and fractal geometry, and its fractal principles are applied in compact antenna design.

### 8. Solid Modelling — NACA Aerofoil

I explored the modelling of an aerofoil profile for an HTOL aircraft using Fusion 360 and a NACA profile tool. I learned about the leading edge, trailing edge, chord line and camber line, which define important aspects of aerofoil geometry. Using a defined NACA profile allows the shape to be generated from established geometric parameters rather than approximated manually. ### 9. Surface Modelling — Toroidal Propeller

I explored the modelling of a toroidal propeller intended for use with a 1000 kV brushless DC (BLDC) motor. The design involved understanding continuous curved blade geometry and the use of surface or freeform modelling tools to create complex shapes. Unlike a conventional open-blade propeller, a toroidal design connects the blade structure into a looped arrangement. I also learned that blade geometry, mass balance, structural integrity and motor compatibility are important considerations before practical operation.

### 11. Organic Design — Hollow Vase

After exploring organic modelling in Fusion 360's Form environment, I understood how freeform modelling can be used to create smooth, curved shapes like a hollow vase. The specified dimensions were a base diameter of 70 mm, an aperture diameter of 55 mm and a wall thickness of 2 mm. I learned how adjusting the form helps create the curved profile and how the opening and wall thickness affect the final shape. Initially, I found this approach different from regular solid modelling, as the shape had to be controlled by adjusting its surfaces rather than relying only on sketches and extrusions. This task helped me understand how Fusion 360 can be used to create organic designs while still following specific dimensions.

### 12. Assembly Prerequisites — Bodies and Components

After exploring bodies and components in Fusion 360, I understood that bodies are mainly used to create the shapes of a model, whereas components are useful when I want to treat different parts independently in an assembly. Initially, I thought both were almost the same, but I realised that the main difference is how they are organised and used when parts need to move or connect with each other. I also learned that naming each body and component properly makes the design easier to understand, especially when working with multiple parts. Creating separate components from the beginning seems like a better approach because it makes adding joints and controlling movement much easier later.


### 13. Assembly and Animation — Mechanical Mechanism

I explored the assembly of mechanical parts using joints and motion tools in Fusion 360. Revolute joints permit rotation about a defined axis, while slider joints permit linear translation along a specified direction. I learned that joints restrict unwanted degrees of freedom and establish the intended movement between components. Motion studies and animation can then be used to observe the mechanism's operation and identify incorrect joint relationships or interference between moving parts.
After studying the quick return mechanism, I understood how rotary motion is converted into reciprocating motion, with the return stroke taking less time than the forward stroke. This mechanism is commonly used in shaping and slotting machines to reduce idle time and improve machining efficiency. I learned how the arrangement of links and joints controls the movement of the ram and creates the difference in stroke times.

### 14. Spur Gear Design

I studied the design of a spur gear pair with a 20-tooth input gear and a 40-tooth output gear. The specified module was 3 mm, the pressure angle was 20°, and steel was the specified material. The gear ratio was calculated from the tooth counts:

Gear ratio: i = Z₂/Z₁ = 40/20 = 2:1

Pitch diameter: d = mZ
- Input: d₁ = 3 × 20 = 60 mm
- Output: d₂ = 3 × 40 = 120 mm

Centre distance: a = (d₁ + d₂)/2 = 90 mm

I learned how the module determines tooth size and how the tooth-count ratio affects rotational speed and torque. Ideally, the output gear rotates at half the input speed, with increased torque before accounting for losses.

### 15. Parametric Design — Box

After working on the parametric box in Fusion 360, I understood how dimensions and parameters can be used to control the geometry of a model. Instead of manually changing individual features, I learned that parameters such as length, width, height and wall thickness can be modified to update the design. I also explored how sketches, constraints and modelling features work together to maintain the intended shape when dimensions change. This helped me understand the importance of planning the model before creating it, as poorly defined constraints can cause unexpected changes in geometry. I found parametric modelling useful because it makes the design easier to modify, saves time when creating different sizes of the same component and reduces the need to rebuild the model from scratch. This approach is particularly useful when designing enclosures, boxes and other mechanical parts that require frequent dimensional changes.
